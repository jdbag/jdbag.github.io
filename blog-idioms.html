50 Essential English Idioms Every Learner Should Know

📅 Your Dictionary Team⏱ 14 min read

Idioms are one of the most challenging — and most rewarding — parts of learning English. They're phrases where the literal meaning of the words is completely different from the actual meaning the speaker intends. When someone says they're "under the weather," they don't mean it's raining on them; they mean they feel ill. When a native speaker says a plan "fell through," nothing physically fell anywhere — the plan simply failed.

For learners, idioms are a double challenge: not only do you need to recognize them and know what they mean, but you also need to know when and how it's appropriate to use them. Using an idiom in the wrong context — too formal, too casual, or with the wrong tone — can be just as confusing as not knowing the idiom at all.

This guide covers 50 of the most common and useful English idioms, organized by category. For each one, you'll find the meaning and a natural example sentence showing how it's actually used.

Idioms About Time and Urgency

1. In the nick of time
Meaning: Just in time; at the last possible moment
"The ambulance arrived in the nick of time — another few minutes and it would have been too late."
2. Bite the bullet
Meaning: To endure a painful or difficult situation with courage
"I hate going to the dentist, but I just had to bite the bullet and make the appointment."
3. Burn the midnight oil
Meaning: To work or study late into the night
"She burned the midnight oil for weeks to finish her thesis before the deadline."
4. Once in a blue moon
Meaning: Very rarely; almost never
"He calls his parents once in a blue moon — maybe twice a year at most."
5. On the spur of the moment
Meaning: Without planning; spontaneously
"On the spur of the moment, we decided to drive to the coast instead of going home."

Idioms About Difficulty and Challenge

6. Hit the nail on the head
Meaning: To describe something exactly correctly; to identify the precise problem
"You hit the nail on the head — the real problem is poor communication between the teams."
7. A piece of cake
Meaning: Something very easy to do
"I was nervous about the exam, but it turned out to be a piece of cake."
8. Bite off more than you can chew
Meaning: To take on more responsibility than you can handle
"She agreed to manage three projects at once and quickly realized she'd bitten off more than she could chew."
9. Between a rock and a hard place
Meaning: Caught between two equally difficult or bad options
"I'm between a rock and a hard place — I can't afford to quit my job, but the working conditions are unbearable."
10. The ball is in your court
Meaning: It's your turn to take action or make a decision
"I've sent the proposal. Now the ball is in their court to decide whether to proceed."

Idioms About Success and Failure

11. Back to the drawing board
Meaning: To start over from the beginning after a plan has failed
"The client rejected our entire concept, so it's back to the drawing board."
12. Burn your bridges
Meaning: To permanently damage a relationship or cut off future options
"Don't quit so angrily — you don't want to burn your bridges with a company you might need later."
13. Get the ball rolling
Meaning: To start a process or activity
"Let's get the ball rolling on this project — who wants to go first?"
14. Miss the boat
Meaning: To miss an opportunity; to be too late
"I should have invested in that company five years ago. I really missed the boat."
15. Hit the ground running
Meaning: To start something quickly and energetically, with immediate success
"She hit the ground running in her new job — within a week she'd already closed two deals."
16. Fall through
Meaning: For a plan or deal to fail or not happen
"We were hoping to buy that apartment, but the deal fell through at the last minute."

Idioms About People and Relationships

17. Rub someone the wrong way
Meaning: To irritate or annoy someone, often unintentionally
"His constant interrupting really rubs me the wrong way."
18. See eye to eye
Meaning: To agree with someone; to have the same opinion
"We don't always see eye to eye on politics, but we respect each other's views."
19. Give someone the cold shoulder
Meaning: To deliberately ignore or be unfriendly to someone
"Ever since their argument last week, she's been giving him the cold shoulder."
20. Cost an arm and a leg
Meaning: To be extremely expensive
"That restaurant was wonderful, but it cost an arm and a leg. We can't afford to go back often."
21. Get on like a house on fire
Meaning: To get along extremely well with someone from the start
"I was nervous about meeting her family, but we got on like a house on fire."
22. Sit on the fence
Meaning: To avoid taking a clear position on something; to be neutral
"Stop sitting on the fence — we need to know which option you support."
"Idioms are the fingerprints of a living language — they reveal its history, humor, and personality in ways that textbooks never can."

Idioms About Knowledge and Understanding

23. Get the hang of something
Meaning: To learn how to do something; to become comfortable with a skill
"This new software is confusing at first, but you'll get the hang of it within a few days."
24. In the dark
Meaning: Uninformed; not knowing something important
"Nobody told me about the meeting — I was completely in the dark until I saw the agenda."
25. Read between the lines
Meaning: To understand a hidden or implied meaning in something
"She didn't say she was unhappy directly, but if you read between the lines, it's pretty clear."
26. Go back to basics
Meaning: To return to fundamental principles or simple methods
"The team has been overthinking the design. Sometimes you need to go back to basics."
27. Put two and two together
Meaning: To figure out something from the available evidence
"When I saw the empty box and the chocolate on his face, I put two and two together."

Idioms About Communication

28. Beat around the bush
Meaning: To avoid saying something directly; to speak vaguely
"Stop beating around the bush and just tell me what the problem is."
29. Spill the beans
Meaning: To reveal a secret or surprise before you're supposed to
"Someone spilled the beans about the surprise party, so she already knows."
30. Let the cat out of the bag
Meaning: To accidentally reveal a secret
"I let the cat out of the bag about the promotion before the official announcement."
31. Speak your mind
Meaning: To say exactly what you think, even if it's not what others want to hear
"I respect that she always speaks her mind, even in difficult meetings."
32. Actions speak louder than words
Meaning: What someone does is more important than what they say
"He promised to change, but actions speak louder than words. Let's see what actually happens."

Idioms About Health and Feelings

33. Under the weather
Meaning: Feeling ill or unwell
"I won't be coming in today — I've been feeling a bit under the weather since yesterday."
34. On top of the world
Meaning: Feeling extremely happy and successful
"After getting the promotion, she felt on top of the world."
35. Get cold feet
Meaning: To become nervous or hesitant about something you planned to do
"He was going to ask her to marry him, but he got cold feet at the last minute."
36. Wear your heart on your sleeve
Meaning: To openly show your emotions without trying to hide them
"She wears her heart on her sleeve — you can always tell exactly how she's feeling."
37. Go through the roof
Meaning: To become extremely angry; also used for prices rising very steeply
"When he found out his car had been damaged, he went through the roof."

Idioms About Work and Effort

38. Pull someone's leg
Meaning: To joke or tease someone
"Are you serious about quitting, or are you just pulling my leg?"
39. Cut corners
Meaning: To do something in a quick, easy way that reduces quality
"The building collapsed because the contractors cut corners on the foundations."
40. Pull your weight
Meaning: To do your fair share of the work
"Everyone in this team needs to pull their weight if we're going to meet the deadline."
41. Think outside the box
Meaning: To think creatively; to approach a problem from a new angle
"We need to think outside the box — the traditional approach clearly isn't working."
42. Go the extra mile
Meaning: To do more than what is required or expected
"What sets great employees apart is their willingness to go the extra mile for clients."

Idioms About Money and Value

43. Break the bank
Meaning: To cost so much that you can't afford it
"We're looking for a good hotel that won't break the bank."
44. Worth its weight in gold
Meaning: Extremely valuable or useful
"A good mentor is worth their weight in gold early in your career."
45. On a shoestring
Meaning: With very little money; on an extremely tight budget
"They built their first business on a shoestring and still managed to turn a profit."

Idioms About Nature and Animals

46. Let sleeping dogs lie
Meaning: To avoid bringing up old problems or conflicts
"That argument was years ago. Let sleeping dogs lie — there's nothing to gain from revisiting it."
47. It's raining cats and dogs
Meaning: To rain very heavily
"You can't go out for a walk now — it's raining cats and dogs out there."
48. Kill two birds with one stone
Meaning: To accomplish two things with a single action
"I'll drop the package off at the post office on my way to the gym — killing two birds with one stone."
49. Take the bull by the horns
Meaning: To directly confront a difficult situation with courage
"Instead of waiting for things to improve, she decided to take the bull by the horns and request a meeting with the director."
50. The straw that broke the camel's back
Meaning: The final problem in a series of problems that finally causes a reaction
"He'd had a difficult week, but that last email was the straw that broke the camel's back — he finally decided to resign."

How to Actually Learn Idioms (Not Just Memorize Them)

Reading a list of idioms and their meanings is useful, but it's only the first step. The real goal is to understand idioms so naturally that you recognize them instantly when you hear them and can use them appropriately in conversation and writing. That requires a different kind of practice than simple memorization.

Notice idioms in real context

Start paying active attention when you encounter idioms in movies, TV shows, podcasts, articles, and books. When you notice one, pause and think about whether the meaning makes sense from the words alone — it almost certainly won't. This active noticing is more valuable than reviewing a list, because it teaches you to recognize the pattern of idiomatic language as something that needs interpretation.

Learn idioms in groups by theme

Idioms about animals, idioms about body parts, idioms about weather, idioms about sport — grouping them thematically makes them easier to remember and easier to find when you need one in conversation. If you're trying to describe a difficult situation and you know you once learned a group of idioms about challenges, you're more likely to recall "between a rock and a hard place" than if you'd learned it in isolation.

Use them carefully — and not too often

One mistake learners make is overusing idioms once they've learned some. Native speakers use idioms fairly naturally and sparingly; conversations aren't usually loaded with figurative language. When learners use too many idioms at once, it can actually sound unnatural or comic rather than fluent. A few well-chosen idioms, used at the right moment, are far more impressive than constant idiomatic expressions used regardless of context.

Be aware of register

Some idioms are casual and informal — "bite the bullet" or "piece of cake" — and wouldn't fit in formal business writing. Others are more neutral. Knowing not just what an idiom means but when it's appropriate to use it is part of advanced fluency.
